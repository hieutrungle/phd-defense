Two placement questions

(a) At a fixed AP count, does a jointly optimized reflector improve priority-region coverage without degrading floor-wide coverage?

(b) Does that improvement reduce the number of APs needed to meet the coverage target?

So far the AP and the reflector were installed in advance, so the gain was bounded by where they were mounted. This part asks where they should go.

It is the same tile array and focal-point steering. Mechanical steering does not depend on the band, so we study indoor Wi-Fi at 5.18 gigahertz, where walls set the AP count; each extra AP costs cabling, power, a license and channel budget.

Positions become design variables, and the reflector, a passive reflective surface, is configured once at installation.

Two questions: (a) better priority coverage at a fixed AP count, and (b) one fewer AP?

Two floorplans with operator-defined priority maps

  • Priority weight $w_u>1$ on rooms that need service, $w_u=1$ elsewhere; weights steer the optimizer only
  • Office: high-priority perimeter rooms shadowed by walls; HRBB: high-priority central block ringed by a corridor
  • 5.18 GHz, 20 dBm, concrete walls; Sionna RT, $10^6$ rays per AP, $1\times1$ m grid at 1.5 m
  • Target: priority-region P5 $\ge-65$ dBm (about 95% of priority locations)

Two university floorplans. Office, 30 by 30 meters, has its highest-priority rooms on the perimeter behind walls. HRBB, a 40 by 40 meter lab floor, has a central priority block.

The operator supplies the priority map: rooms that must be served weigh more than corridors. Weights only shape the objective; reported RSSI is unweighted.

The target: priority-region fifth percentile at least minus 65 dBm.

Design vector: AP poses, wall position, focal points

Bisector rule with virtual focal points
$$\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{ij}=\frac{\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{\,\mathrm{in}}_{ij}+\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{\,\mathrm{out}}_{ij}}{\big\|\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{\,\mathrm{in}}_{ij}+\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{\,\mathrm{out}}_{ij}\big\|_2}$$

Unit vectors from tile $(i,j)$ toward $\mathbf{f}_1$, $\mathbf{f}_2$; $\mathbf{f}_1=$ AP, $\mathbf{f}_2=$ user gives the bisector rule.

  • AP $n$: position $\mathbf{p}_n$ at ceiling height $h_{\mathrm{ceil}}=3.8$ m; downward boresight $\mathbf{o}_n=\mathbf{v}_n/\|\mathbf{v}_n\|_2$
  • Reflector centroid $(u_{\mathrm{ref}},v_{\mathrm{ref}})\in[0,1]^2$ on the mounting region $\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{wall}}$ of one preselected wall
  • Virtual source $\mathbf{f}_1$, virtual target $\mathbf{f}_2$, set once at installation: 6 coordinates instead of $2N_rN_c=126$ tile angles
  • Feasible set $\mathcal{M}$: floorplan bounds, $d_{\min}=5$ m AP separation (clipping, push-apart); tile angles clipped to $\pm30^\circ$
Joint design vector ($5N+8$ free coordinates)
$$\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\{\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{p}_N,\mathbf{v}_N,u_{\mathrm{ref}},v_{\mathrm{ref}},\mathbf{f}_1,\mathbf{f}_2\}$$

Everything we can choose goes into one continuous design vector, Theta.

Each AP sits on the ceiling. We optimize its horizontal position and an orientation vector normalized to a downward boresight, which avoids the singularities of Euler angles.

The reflector, 63 hexagonal tiles over 2 by 2 meters, slides along one preselected wall through two normalized coordinates.

Its tiles are steered by a virtual source and a virtual target: each tile normal bisects the directions to them, so placing them at the AP and a user recovers the bisector rule. Six focal coordinates replace 126 tile angles, so Theta has 5N plus 8 coordinates.

Objective: soft worst case plus floor-wide coverage

Strongest-server power, penalty, and objective
$$\begin{aligned} P_{u,\mathrm{net}}&=\max_{n}P_{u,n}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\\[2pt] s_u&=\tfrac{x_u-\mathrm{floor}_{\mathrm{dBm}}}{\mathrm{ceil}_{\mathrm{dBm}}-\mathrm{floor}_{\mathrm{dBm}}},\quad p_u=1-\min(s_u,1)\\[2pt] \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{softmin}}&=T_{\mathrm{sm}}\Big[\log\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}w_u e^{p_u/T_{\mathrm{sm}}}-\log\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}w_u\Big]\\[2pt] \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coverage}}&=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{U}|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\sigma\Big(\frac{x_u-\gamma_{\mathrm{SLA}}}{T_{\mathrm{cov}}}\Big)\\[2pt] \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}&=\alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{softmin}}+\beta\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coverage}} \end{aligned}$$
  • $P_{u,n}$: ray-traced radio map, incoherent sum of path powers; $x_u$: $P_{u,\mathrm{net}}$ in dBm
  • $T_{\mathrm{sm}}\to0$: worst-location penalty; $T_{\mathrm{sm}}\to\infty$: weighted mean
  • Weight $w_u$ acts as a penalty offset $T_{\mathrm{sm}}\ln w_u$
  • $\gamma_{\mathrm{SLA}}$: service target; the coverage term has a small weight: it encourages, but does not guarantee, floor-wide coverage

Weights, temperatures and score limits: backup

Each location is served by its strongest AP, and its power maps to a penalty that grows as power drops.

An average hides holes and a hard minimum is not smooth, so the main term is a priority-weighted soft worst case; a larger weight acts like a lower RSSI.

A small sigmoid term discourages sacrificing the rest of the floor.

Two-phase method: PSO search, then gradient refinement

Why gradients alone fall short

  • Paths appear and vanish at visibility boundaries
  • Walls split the design space into separate good regions
  • Each location's gradient reaches only its serving AP

PSO+GD

  • Phase 1: PSO; each particle is a full layout
  • Phase 2: Adam steps through differentiable Sionna RT
Computational cost per run
About 180 s of serial work per run on one RTX 5070 Ti GPU (swarm and gradient phases; settings in backup)

The objective is differentiable for a fixed set of paths, but a small move of an AP or the reflector can create or remove paths at a visibility boundary, and walls split the design space into separated good regions.

So we use two phases. First, particle swarm optimization; each particle is a complete layout scored by a full radio map. PSO moves whole configurations, whereas GA crossover could split an AP from the reflector it works with.

Then the best particle is refined with Adam steps through the differentiable ray tracer. One run costs about 180 seconds of serial GPU work.

Intuition: a swarm of layouts, then gradient refinement

Illustrative

Schematic floorplan and toy propagation model, not simulation output. Each particle = one layout: three APs, reflector position, focal target.

Here is the intuition, as a schematic. Each color is one particle, a full layout of three APs and the reflector.

Every iteration, a particle keeps part of its velocity and is pulled toward its own best layout, the hollow rings, and toward the swarm's best, the large AP icons. The heat map is the coverage of that best layout, and the inset curve its objective.

Once the swarm settles, the label switches to gradient refinement: small deterministic steps move the best layout locally to lift the weakest priority rooms.

Evaluation protocol
  • Office and HRBB, with and without the PRS
  • 1 to 7 APs; 5 seeds per configuration
  • Metrics: mean, P5 and minimum RSSI, over priority regions and over the whole floor
  • Target: priority-region P5 $\ge-65$ dBm

Five baselines each remove or replace one component. GA+GD swaps the global search. PSO-only skips refinement. Random+GD removes global search. Weighted k-means ignores walls. Random sampling is the floor.

Each configuration runs with five seeds, one to seven APs, both floorplans, with and without the reflector. I report mean, P5 and minimum RSSI, with Welch tests and 95 percent intervals.

Limitations
  • Strongest-server received power only: no interference, SINR, capacity, or installed cost
  • Five seeds, two floorplans, one preselected wall per floor (an unquantified PRS advantage)
  • Known geometry, incoherent power sum, no measured reflector; new search per floorplan
Answered (WHERE): joint AP and reflector planning met the priority target with one fewer AP in both layouts (seed mean, small margins).
Opens (WHICH): how do placement and operation fit together, and which method fits which problem? → conclusions.

The maps show where the gain comes from. PSO+GD places APs in a west room and at two corridor junctions and reaches shadowed rooms through corridors and doorways; the reflector serves the north rooms. Weighted k-means follows Euclidean distance and leaves the southeast dark.

The limits: strongest-server received power only, so the AP saving is a coverage statement, not a cost statement; five seeds, two floorplans, one preselected wall, known geometry.

Within those limits, joint planning removed one AP in both layouts, as a seed mean with small margins. That answers where.

The complete picture

Back to the map from the start, now with the answers filled in. Each part has its own band and scene, so these gains are not compared across parts.

What: geometry alone raises path gain by 53.0 and 87.5 dB for one static user.

How: multi-agent learning adds 20.7 dB over a static flat reflector, and learning the assignment lifts four users from minus 72.46 to minus 66.62 dBm over a fixed split.

Where: with three APs, placement raises priority P5 by 4.8 dB, and one fewer AP meets the target, as a seed mean.

Limitations and next steps

Limitations
  • Ray-traced simulation only; no fabricated-array validation
  • Received power, not rate; no interference model
  • Few scenes, user counts, seeds, and mounting walls
  • Path length and phase not controlled; no position-tracking geometric baseline for learned control
Next steps
  • Hardware-in-the-loop tests on servo arrays
  • Training under mobility and unseen localization error
  • SINR/rate-aware, multi-AP objectives
  • Run a placed layout with the learned controller (the dashed arrow)

The limits apply to every part: ray-traced simulation only, received power rather than rate, few scenarios. Phase is not controlled, and learned control was not compared with a position-tracking geometric rule.

Next: hardware-in-the-loop tests, training under mobility, rate-aware objectives, and running a placed layout with the learned controller, the dashed arrow.

Five questions, answered

WHYSpatial information suffices: every part decides from user positions (run time) or a site model (planning), never from pilot-based CSI estimation; the cost is decimeter-level localization and site-model fidelity.
WHATFocal points are the shared interface: one abstraction steers the tiles in the geometric rule, both learned controllers, and the placement optimizer.
HOWLearned, decentralized policies act within servo limits and are trained on received power for several moving users; when segments differ in geometry, learning the assignment beats a fixed split and the best geometric heuristic.
WHEREWhere to install is a decision of its own: a reflector configured once and placed jointly with the APs met the priority-coverage target with one fewer AP (seed mean, small margins).
WHICHMatch the method to the problem. Geometry for one static user or as a warm start; learned policies for run-time multi-user control; direct optimization (PSO + gradient refinement) for once-per-building placement.
Together, the answers cover both stages, where to install and how to operate, through one focal-point interface and without pilot-based CSI estimation (ray-traced simulation); closing the loop between them is the next step.

To sum up, what the five answers say together.

Why: spatial information is enough. Every part decides from user positions at run time or a site model at planning, never from pilot-based channel estimation; the price is decimeter-level localization and an accurate site model.

What: the focal point is the interface shared by the geometric rule, both learned controllers and the placement optimizer.

How: learned decentralized policies work within servo limits for several moving users, and when segments differ in geometry, learning the assignment beats a fixed split and the geometric heuristic.

Where: installation is a decision of its own; a jointly placed reflector met the priority-coverage target with one fewer access point.

Which: geometry for a static user or a warm start, learned policies at run time, direct optimization once per building.

Together these cover installing and operating the reflectors through one interface, in simulation; closing the loop between the two stages and hardware validation come next.
