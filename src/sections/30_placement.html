Two placement questions

(a) At a fixed AP count, does a jointly optimized reflector improve priority-region coverage without degrading floor-wide coverage?

(b) Does that improvement reduce the number of APs needed to meet the coverage target?

Suppose we're planning Wi-Fi for a building before any hardware goes in. We can choose the access point locations, where to mount one reflector, and how to orient its tiles. The tile settings stay fixed after installation.

I'll ask two questions. With the same number of access points, can the reflector improve coverage in the rooms that need it most? And if it does, can we meet the coverage target with one fewer access point?

Two floorplans with operator-defined priority maps

  • Priority weight $w_u>1$ on rooms that need service, $w_u=1$ elsewhere; weights steer the optimizer only
  • Office: high-priority perimeter rooms shadowed by walls; HRBB: high-priority central block ringed by a corridor
  • 5.18 GHz, 20 dBm, concrete walls; Sionna RT, $10^6$ rays per AP, $1\times1$ m grid at 1.5 m
  • Target: priority-region P5 $\ge-65$ dBm (about 95% of priority locations)

In the Office floor plan, the high-priority rooms sit along the perimeter. In the HRBB lab, they form a central block. The colored maps tell the optimizer which rooms to emphasize. We evaluate the actual signal strength there. Our target is a fifth percentile, or P5, of minus 65 dBm. That means roughly 95 percent of priority locations reach that level or higher.

Design vector: AP poses, wall position, focal points

Bisector rule with virtual focal points
$$\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{ij}=\frac{\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{\,\mathrm{in}}_{ij}+\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{\,\mathrm{out}}_{ij}}{\big\|\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{\,\mathrm{in}}_{ij}+\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{\,\mathrm{out}}_{ij}\big\|_2}$$

Unit vectors from tile $(i,j)$ toward $\mathbf{f}_1$, $\mathbf{f}_2$; $\mathbf{f}_1=$ AP, $\mathbf{f}_2=$ user gives the bisector rule.

  • Each AP: position on the ceiling (3.8 m) and pointing direction
  • Reflector position: can move along a preselected wall
  • Reflector control: 2 focal points (6 coordinates instead of 126 tile angles)
  • Constraints: inside the floorplan, APs at least 5 m apart, tile angles within $\pm30^\circ$
Joint design vector ($5N+8$ free coordinates)
$$\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\{\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{p}_N,\mathbf{v}_N,u_{\mathrm{ref}},v_{\mathrm{ref}},\mathbf{f}_1,\mathbf{f}_2\}$$

A candidate layout specifies where each ceiling access point sits and which way it points. It also specifies where the reflector sits on a preselected wall. We can move the panel along that wall and change its aim.

The two blue points in the diagram determine that aim. For each tile, we use one point as a virtual source and the other as a virtual target. The tile normal bisects those two directions. Six coordinates specify the orientation of all 63 tiles, so the optimizer can search over the entire panel without choosing 126 angles individually.

Objective: soft worst case plus floor-wide coverage

Strongest-server power, penalty, and objective
$$\begin{aligned} P_{u,\mathrm{net}}&=\max_{n}P_{u,n}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\\[2pt] s_u&=\tfrac{x_u-\mathrm{floor}_{\mathrm{dBm}}}{\mathrm{ceil}_{\mathrm{dBm}}-\mathrm{floor}_{\mathrm{dBm}}},\quad p_u=1-\min(s_u,1)\\[2pt] \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{softmin}}&=T_{\mathrm{sm}}\Big[\log\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}w_u e^{p_u/T_{\mathrm{sm}}}-\log\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}w_u\Big]\\[2pt] \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coverage}}&=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{U}|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\sigma\Big(\frac{x_u-\gamma_{\mathrm{SLA}}}{T_{\mathrm{cov}}}\Big)\\[2pt] \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}&=\alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{softmin}}+\beta\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coverage}} \end{aligned}$$
  • $P_{u,n}$: ray-traced radio map, incoherent sum of path powers; $x_u$: $P_{u,\mathrm{net}}$ in dBm
  • $T_{\mathrm{sm}}\to0$: worst-location penalty; $T_{\mathrm{sm}}\to\infty$: weighted mean
  • Weight $w_u$ acts as a penalty offset $T_{\mathrm{sm}}\ln w_u$
  • $\gamma_{\mathrm{SLA}}$: service target; the coverage term has a small weight: it encourages, but does not guarantee, floor-wide coverage

Weights, temperatures and score limits: backup

Imagine a layout with strong coverage in the corridor and a weak signal in a priority room. A floor average can hide that room. We take the strongest access point at each location, convert weak power into a penalty, and use a weighted, smooth approximation to the worst penalty. A smaller, floor-wide term rewards layouts that bring more locations above the service threshold.

Two-phase method: PSO search, then gradient refinement

Why not gradients alone?

  • The coverage landscape is not convex: many local minima
  • Gradient descent alone settles at a nearby minimum and usually misses the best layout
  • PSO first finds a good coarse position

PSO+GD

  • Phase 1: PSO; each particle is a full layout
  • Phase 2: Adam steps through differentiable Sionna RT

The coverage landscape is not convex. Walls and doorways create many local minima, so gradient descent alone settles at a nearby minimum and usually misses the best layout. That's why we start with a global search.

We use particle swarm optimization, or PSO. Each particle represents a complete layout, and a ray-traced power map gives it a score. Then we take the best layout and refine it with Adam, using gradients through the ray tracer.

When we optimize the reflector and access points together, it rises to minus 57.3. The gain is 4.8 decibels, with a 95 percent confidence interval from 3.1 to 6.5.

The weakest priority location gains even more, about 9.5 decibels. The floor-wide statistics below also improve. So the benefit reaches the locations we set out to help.

Coverage maps and limitations

Office, 3 APs (red), PRS on the north wall; color scale $-80$ to $-40$ dBm

PSO+GD reaches shadowed rooms through corridors and doorways; weighted $k$-means leaves southeast shadows.

Limitations
  • Strongest-server received power only: no interference, SINR, capacity, or installed cost
  • Five seeds, two floorplans, one preselected wall per floor (an unquantified PRS advantage)
  • Known geometry, incoherent power sum, no measured reflector; new search per floorplan
Answered (WHERE): joint AP and reflector planning met the priority target with one fewer AP in both layouts (seed mean, small margins).
Opens (WHICH): how do placement and operation fit together, and which method fits which problem? → conclusions.

The red dots show where the access points ended up. In the left map, two sit near corridor junctions, where paths can reach several rooms through doorways. The right map uses weighted k-means. A nearby access point can still be on the wrong side of a wall, as the darker southeast rooms illustrate.

These are strongest-server power maps. They tell us about coverage. To translate one fewer access point into a deployment decision, we'd also need interference, traffic demand, and installed cost. We tested two floor plans with one candidate wall on each.

The complete picture

Read this map in the order an engineer would use it. Before installation, the site model helps choose access point and reflector locations. In the Office, that choice raised the fifth percentile in priority rooms by 4.8 decibels with three access points. Once the hardware is installed, the decision changes. As users move, the controller chooses who each segment serves and where it aims.

The common interface is the focal point. The planning and control results come from separate experiments at different frequencies. The dashed arrow is how we would connect them in a single deployment.

Limitations and next steps

Limitations
  • Ray-traced simulation only; no fabricated-array validation
  • Received power, not rate; no interference model
  • Few scenes, user counts, seeds, and mounting walls
  • Path length and phase not controlled; no position-tracking geometric baseline for learned control
Next steps
  • Hardware-in-the-loop tests on servo arrays
  • Training under mobility and unseen localization error
  • SINR/rate-aware, multi-AP objectives
  • Run a placed layout with the learned controller (the dashed arrow)

All the results I've shown come from ray tracing. We evaluated path gain or received power, and the tiles steer directions without controlling phase. Two comparisons would be particularly informative next. One is to run geometric tracking and learned control on the same moving users. The other is to operate a jointly planned layout on real hardware and measure rate under interference.

Five questions, answered

WHYSpatial information suffices: every part decides from user positions (run time) or a site model (planning), never from pilot-based CSI estimation; the cost is decimeter-level localization and site-model fidelity.
WHATFocal points are the shared interface: one abstraction steers the tiles in the geometric rule, both learned controllers, and the placement optimizer.
HOWLearned, decentralized policies act within servo limits and are trained on received power for several moving users; when segments differ in geometry, learning the assignment beats a fixed split and the best geometric heuristic.
WHEREWhere to install is a decision of its own: a reflector configured once and placed jointly with the APs met the priority-coverage target with one fewer AP (seed mean, small margins).
WHICHMatch the method to the problem. Geometry for one static user or as a warm start; learned policies for run-time multi-user control; direct optimization (PSO + gradient refinement) for once-per-building placement.
Together, the answers cover both stages, where to install and how to operate, through one focal-point interface and without pilot-based CSI estimation (ray-traced simulation); closing the loop between them is the next step.

I'll return to the user around the corner. If we know where that user is, the bisector rule tells us how to orient a tile. A focal point lets us use that same construction even when the best place to aim is a decision we need to learn.

With several users, we can learn a local steering policy for each segment. When the segments see the room from different angles, it also pays to learn their assignments. Before installation, when the building is known and the mounting sites are still open, we can optimize the layout directly.

Across these studies, the reflector decisions use positions at run time or a site model at planning time. The price of avoiding per-element channel estimation is that localization and model accuracy now matter. The next test is to bring placement and control together in a physical room.
