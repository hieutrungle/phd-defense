Two placement questions

(a) At a fixed AP count, does a jointly optimized reflector improve priority-region coverage without degrading floor-wide coverage?

(b) Does that improvement reduce the number of APs needed to meet the coverage target?

Consider a building before the access points go in. Each additional access point needs power, cabling, and a place in the channel plan. Could a passive panel cover a difficult room by redirecting power from an access point we already plan to install? Here we set the panel's aim once, at installation.

I'll test that in two stages. At a fixed access-point count, does adding one jointly placed reflector help the weakest priority locations while maintaining coverage elsewhere? Then, if coverage improves, is the margin large enough to remove an access point and still meet the target?

Two floorplans with operator-defined priority maps

  • Priority weight $w_u>1$ on rooms that need service, $w_u=1$ elsewhere; weights steer the optimizer only
  • Office: high-priority perimeter rooms shadowed by walls; HRBB: high-priority central block ringed by a corridor
  • 5.18 GHz, 20 dBm, concrete walls; Sionna RT, $10^6$ rays per AP, $1\times1$ m grid at 1.5 m
  • Target: priority-region P5 $\ge-65$ dBm (about 95% of priority locations)

For planning, we move to 5.18 gigahertz and two floor plans. In the Office, priority rooms line the perimeter. In HRBB, they form a central block. The weights tell the optimizer where service matters most. We judge the result in dBm. A fifth percentile, or P5, of minus 65 dBm means roughly 95 percent of priority locations reach that level or better.

Design vector: AP poses, wall position, focal points

Bisector rule with virtual focal points
$$\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{ij}=\frac{\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{\,\mathrm{in}}_{ij}+\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{\,\mathrm{out}}_{ij}}{\big\|\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{\,\mathrm{in}}_{ij}+\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{\,\mathrm{out}}_{ij}\big\|_2}$$

Unit vectors from tile $(i,j)$ toward $\mathbf{f}_1$, $\mathbf{f}_2$; $\mathbf{f}_1=$ AP, $\mathbf{f}_2=$ user gives the bisector rule.

  • Each AP: position on the ceiling (3.8 m) and pointing direction
  • Reflector position: can move along a preselected wall
  • Reflector control: 2 focal points (6 coordinates instead of 126 tile angles)
  • Constraints: inside the floorplan, APs at least 5 m apart, tile angles within $\pm30^\circ$
Joint design vector ($5N+8$ free coordinates)
$$\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\{\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{p}_N,\mathbf{v}_N,u_{\mathrm{ref}},v_{\mathrm{ref}},\mathbf{f}_1,\mathbf{f}_2\}$$

What can the planner move to meet that target? Each access point gets a ceiling position and a pointing direction. The reflector can move along a preselected wall. The two focal points in the drawing determine its tile angles. From a tile, draw one arrow to the virtual source and one to the virtual target. The tile normal bisects those directions just as in the hallway.

Six coordinates therefore set 126 tile angles. We also enforce access-point separation and the thirty-degree servo limits. Now we need a way to rank those feasible layouts.

Objective: soft worst case plus floor-wide coverage

Strongest-server power, penalty, and objective
$$\begin{aligned} P_{u,\mathrm{net}}&=\max_{n}P_{u,n}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\\[2pt] s_u&=\tfrac{x_u-\mathrm{floor}_{\mathrm{dBm}}}{\mathrm{ceil}_{\mathrm{dBm}}-\mathrm{floor}_{\mathrm{dBm}}},\quad p_u=1-\min(s_u,1)\\[2pt] \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{softmin}}&=T_{\mathrm{sm}}\Big[\log\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}w_u e^{p_u/T_{\mathrm{sm}}}-\log\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}w_u\Big]\\[2pt] \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coverage}}&=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{U}|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\sigma\Big(\frac{x_u-\gamma_{\mathrm{SLA}}}{T_{\mathrm{cov}}}\Big)\\[2pt] \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}&=\alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{softmin}}+\beta\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coverage}} \end{aligned}$$
  • $P_{u,n}$: ray-traced radio map, incoherent sum of path powers; $x_u$: $P_{u,\mathrm{net}}$ in dBm
  • $T_{\mathrm{sm}}\to0$: worst-location penalty; $T_{\mathrm{sm}}\to\infty$: weighted mean
  • Weight $w_u$ acts as a penalty offset $T_{\mathrm{sm}}\ln w_u$
  • $\gamma_{\mathrm{SLA}}$: service target; the coverage term has a small weight: it encourages, but does not guarantee, floor-wide coverage

Weights, temperatures and score limits: backup

Imagine moving an access point from a strong corridor toward a weak priority room. To score that move, we take the strongest access point at each grid cell. The first term puts more weight on weak locations and on priority rooms. The second rewards bringing more of the whole floor above threshold. We then search for layouts that reduce this combined loss.

Two-phase method: PSO search, then gradient refinement

PSO+GD

  • Phase 1: PSO; each particle is a full layout
  • Phase 2: Adam steps through differentiable Sionna RT

Why not gradients alone?

  • The coverage landscape is not convex: many local minima
  • Gradient descent alone settles at a nearby minimum and usually misses the best layout
  • PSO first finds a good coarse position

Particle swarm optimization, or PSO, first compares complete layouts in different parts of the floor plan. We take the best layout it finds and refine it with Adam, following gradients through the ray tracer.

Why start with a swarm? A small change in antenna direction might help a room. Moving the access point to a different doorway could help much more, but a local gradient may never lead us there. The animation shows how the swarm finds a layout and hands it over for refinement.

Start with the priority P5 row. We use the same planning method and keep three access points in both columns. Adding the jointly optimized reflector raises P5 from minus 62.1 to minus 57.3 dBm. That's a 4.8 dB gain across five seeds, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 3.1 to 6.5 dB. The weakest priority location gains even more, 9.5 dB. The whole-floor statistics below improve as well.

The bars on the right show why placement matters. With weighted k-means the same reflector adds only 1.3 dB to priority P5. Both global searches followed by refinement realize larger gains, and the five runs leave their relative ranking uncertain.

That answers the fixed-count question. Both three-access-point layouts already clear minus 65 dBm. The practical question is what happens if we take one access point away.

Why PSO+GD covers more
  • PSO+GD scores every candidate layout with the ray tracer, so it sees the walls
  • It places APs near corridor junctions, where signals reach several rooms through doorways
  • Weighted $k$-means uses only distance and priority and ignores walls
  • A nearby AP can sit on the wrong side of a wall: the darker southeast rooms
Answered (WHERE): joint AP and reflector planning met the priority target with one fewer AP in both layouts.
Opens (WHICH): how do placement and operation fit together, and which method fits which problem? → conclusions.

That explains why the building model matters at installation. We can now put placement back alongside the decision about how to steer.

The complete picture

Read this diagram as an engineer making two decisions. Before installation, on the left, use the building model to search for access-point and reflector locations, then refine the best layout. After installation, on the right, the decision depends on who is moving through the room. A segment can learn where to aim, and an allocator can learn when to hand a user to another segment.

Underneath both is the same geometric operation. Given a focal point, turn the tiles toward it. The dashed arrow points to future work. We could use operating experience to revisit the original plan.

What comes next: the FORMULA3 project

FORMULA3 · NSF–Research Council of Finland VINES
Framework for Optimal Resource Management Using Generalizable AI in FR3 Dynamic Programmable Wireless Environments
  • VINES: Verticals-enabling Intelligent Network Systems; collaborative effort of about $1.54M, Oct 2026 – Sep 2029
  • Five universities: Tennessee Tech, Texas A&M, NYU, Tampere University, Aalto University

Programmable environments combine RIS with metallic reflector arrays: hexagonal metal-tile panels whose tiles turn so the panel focuses on the user. This element builds on this dissertation's reflector-array work (hardware and focal-point control).

U.S. National Science Foundation (NSF) Award Nos. 2549087, 2549088 · Research Council of Finland Decision Nos. 380594, 380600 · vines-formula3.github.io

FORMULA3 takes these ideas from simulated rooms to a physical reflector. It's a collaboration among five universities in the United States and Finland, supported by the National Science Foundation and the Research Council of Finland. The project asks how AI can manage wireless resources in the FR3 frequency range, when parts of the environment can be reconfigured.

The connection to this dissertation is the panel on the right. The project combines electronic surfaces with mechanical reflector arrays. We can bring our tile model and focal-point controller to that effort. Here's the physical device the project will use.
