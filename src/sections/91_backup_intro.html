Limitations and next steps

Limitations
  • Ray-traced simulation only; no fabricated-array validation
  • Received power, not rate; no interference model
  • Few scenes, user counts, seeds, and mounting walls
  • Path length and phase not controlled; no position-tracking geometric baseline for learned control
Next steps
  • Hardware-in-the-loop tests on servo arrays
  • Training under mobility and unseen localization error
  • SINR/rate-aware, multi-AP objectives
  • Run a placed layout with the learned controller (the dashed arrow)

All the results I've shown come from ray tracing. We evaluated path gain or received power, and the tiles steer directions without controlling phase. Two comparisons would be particularly informative next. One is to run geometric tracking and learned control on the same moving users. The other is to operate a jointly planned layout on real hardware and measure rate under interference.

Research and technical innovation

Geometric foundations

Bisector rule and focal points steer metallic tiles from positions alone.
WHAT: what geometry alone achieves

Flat MARL control

Multi-agent RL that maps servo-actuated tile orientations to a few virtual focal points.
HOW: steer the array for moving users

Hierarchical MARL

Two-timescale allocator + MAPPO segment agents, with no pilot-based CSI estimation.
HOW: share the array among several users

Joint placement

Access points and a reflector placed jointly: PSO global search + gradient refinement through a differentiable ray tracer.
WHERE: install the access points and the reflector

Four decisions connect the studies. Geometry tells us how to turn a tile. A local policy chooses where a segment aims. A higher-level policy decides which user that segment serves. The placement optimizer chooses where the access points and reflector go before operation begins. Focal points give all four methods a way to specify the reflection they want.

T-shaped hallway: RSSI at nine UE locations

  • Best reflector case at all nine locations, but below free space at locations 2 and 5
  • Likely explanation, not verified in the original study: the rule aligns path directions but not lengths, so phases are uncontrolled

At all nine positions in the T-shaped hallway, focusing gives the strongest signal among the reflector configurations. At positions two and five, the free-space reference is still higher. The bisector rule aligns the directions of the reflected paths. Their lengths can differ, so their phases can differ at the receiver. That's a possible explanation for the dips. A phase-resolved comparison would be needed to test it.

We use ITU Recommendation P.2040 to assign electrical properties to the scene materials. The two equations give relative permittivity and conductivity as functions of carrier frequency. bisector rule in 3D: tile elevation and azimuth

$$\vec n=\frac12\left(\frac{\overrightarrow{AB}}{|\overrightarrow{AB}|}+\frac{\overrightarrow{AC}}{|\overrightarrow{AC}|}\right)$$ $$\theta=\arccos\frac{z}{|\vec n|},\qquad \phi=\mathrm{sgn}(y)\arccos\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}$$
  • With the focal-point abstraction, C is replaced by the segment's focal point $f_l$
  • Learned control clips $\theta,\phi$ to the servo range

Start at point A, the center of a tile. Form a unit vector toward the access point and another toward the receiver. Their sum points along the required surface normal. Its direction gives the two angles the tile needs. With focal-point control, we use the virtual target in place of the receiver when we form the second vector.
