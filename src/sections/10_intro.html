Section divider: why steer reflectors from spatial information instead of channel estimates?

Growing demand points to mmWave bandwidth

  • Growing traffic (AR, autonomous systems, IoT) needs more bandwidth or better signal quality
  • Large contiguous channels are available at mmWave
Image credit: spectrummattersindeed.blogspot.com/2020/01/why-millimeter-wave-is-essential-for-5g.html

Each wireless generation carries more demanding applications: augmented reality, autonomous systems, large-scale IoT. Engineers have two levers: more bandwidth, or better signal quality.

The large contiguous channels are at millimeter-wave frequencies, where propagation is mostly line of sight.

More bandwidth at mmWave, but less coverage

  • The cost: higher path loss, weak penetration, frequent blockage
  • The environment (walls, materials, shadowing) is usually a given channel to compensate for
Image credit: cdn.codeground.org/nsr/downloads/researchareas/2022May_6G_Spectrum.pdf

This chart shows the trade-off: higher frequency, more bandwidth, smaller coverage.

The price is higher path loss, weak penetration and frequent blockage. Usually the environment is a fixed channel to compensate for; this dissertation asks what happens if part of it becomes controllable.

Target applications: dense, shielded indoor environments

Data centers

Metal server racks block signals; high data rates are needed without running fiber to every rack.

Nuclear facilities

Radiation-shielded telemetry behind thick concrete and metal, where wiring and delicate electronics are prone to failure.

Industrial plants

Connectivity for autonomous robots and sensors across cluttered manufacturing geometries.

In all three, links are blocked by structure, so coverage depends on reaching non-line-of-sight (NLOS) users.

Three settings motivate this work: data centers, where metal racks block signals; nuclear facilities, where telemetry must work behind shielding; and industrial plants, with robots in cluttered geometry.

In all three, structure blocks the direct path, so coverage depends on reaching non-line-of-sight users. The scenes later in the talk are generic indoor geometries with this blockage.

Indoor mmWave links are blocked by structure

  • At mmWave, NLOS links suffer severe path loss and blockage
  • Conventional compensation: denser infrastructure, higher transmit power, more complex signal processing
  • Each adds energy consumption and deployment cost
  • Smart radio environments: make parts of the propagation environment itself controllable
The corner blocks the direct path; a reflector creates an NLOS path into the dead zone

The core challenge in one picture: in an L-shaped corridor the corner blocks the direct path, and the user around the corner sits in a dead zone.

The usual answer is more access points, more power, or more processing, each adding cost. The first remedy returns in placement. The alternative: a controllable surface at the corner creates a reflected path into the dead zone.

The CSI bottleneck: pilots grow with elements × users

Illustrative
Electronic RIS: the cascaded channel of every element to every user must be estimated; pilot time grows with $N \times K$ and eats the coherence window.
Mechanical reflector array: the controller needs only the $K$ user positions; its input does not grow with the number of tiles $N$.

Why avoid channel estimation? On the left is an electronic RIS with N elements serving K users. To configure it, the controller needs the cascaded channel through every element to every user, so pilot time grows with N times K.

Watch the bar: the channel stays coherent only for a fixed window. As the surface grows, pilots fill more of it and little time is left for data.

On the right, a mechanical reflector array is steered from user positions. Its control input stays small, independent of the number of tiles. This is schematic, not a measurement.

Electronic RIS vs. metallic reflector arrays

Electronic RISMetallic reflector array (this work)
SteeringPIN or varactor phase shifters per elementServo-rotated metallic tiles, specular reflection
BandUnit cells designed for one bandTiles large vs. wavelength: same geometry across bands
Control inputCascaded CSI per element; pilots grow with elements × usersRun time: user positions only; training: received power as the reward; no pilot-based CSI estimation
PowerBias voltage to hold the stateNone once positioned; only while actuators move
LimitationHardware cost and CSI overhead at scaleSlower reconfiguration: quasi-static indoor scenes
The burden shifts from channel estimation to localization (run time) and the site model (planning).

The standard controllable surface, the electronic RIS, faces three barriers: unit cells designed for one band; the cascaded channel through every element, which we just saw; and bias power to hold its state.

Our alternative is a metallic array of servo-rotated tiles, large compared with the wavelength, so they act as mirrors across bands.

At run time the controller uses only user positions; received power serves as the training reward. The tiles draw no power once positioned. The trade-off is slower reconfiguration, which suits quasi-static indoor scenes.

Dissertation goal and research questions

The thesis, at the top: spatial information, meaning AP and tile positions, user-position estimates and a site model, is enough to configure, control and place these reflectors without pilot-based channel estimation. That is what spatial intelligence means here; the cost moves to localization and site-model accuracy.

The numbers give the talk order: what geometry achieves, how to operate the array, then where to install it, because the controllers take the mounting sites as given. The map itself is in system order.

The two stages share the focal-point interface. They were studied separately, and coupling them, the dashed arrow, is future work.

Section divider: what hardware and abstraction, and what does geometry alone achieve?

Hardware: metallic linear Fresnel reflector (LFR) arrays

  • $N_r \times N_c$ metallic tiles, each rotated in elevation $\theta$ and azimuth $\phi$
  • Redirects the incident wavefront by specular reflection; no RF control circuitry
  • Geometric study: $10 \times 7$ square tiles of 20 cm × 20 cm (1.4 m × 2.0 m), unconstrained rotation

The hardware throughout is a metallic linear Fresnel reflector array: a grid of metallic tiles, each rotated in elevation and azimuth.

It redirects the wave by ordinary specular reflection, so it needs no RF circuitry. In the top view, each tile is angled so its reflection from the access point lands on the user.

The geometric study uses ten by seven square tiles, a 1.4 by 2.0 meter device, with unconstrained rotation.

Bisector rule: each tile normal splits AP and user directions

Illustrative

Tile center A, AP at B, user at C. Law of reflection:

$$\vec n=\tfrac12\Big(\tfrac{\overrightarrow{AB}}{|\overrightarrow{AB}|}+\tfrac{\overrightarrow{AC}}{|\overrightarrow{AC}|}\Big)$$

Only positions enter; no channel estimate.

Normals: angle in = angle out at every tile, so all reflected rays meet at the user.
Servo limits: a tile that needs more than $\pm30^\circ$ is clipped (red); its ray misses the user.

How is each tile oriented? This is a top view: the access point on the left, a row of tiles on the wall, and a user walking along the hallway.

Call the tile center A, the access point B and the user C. By the law of reflection the tile normal must bisect the unit vectors toward the access point and toward the user. As the user moves, every tile turns, and all reflected rays still converge on the user.

First click: the dashed normals, with equal angles on each side. Only positions enter this rule; there is no channel estimate.

Second click: real servos have a limited range. When the user walks back toward the access point side, some tiles would need more than 30 degrees. They clip, turn red, and their rays miss.

Focal-point abstraction: a segment is steered by 3 coordinates

Illustrative
All tiles of segment $l$ aim at one virtual focal point $f_l \in \mathbb{R}^3$ instead of the user; angles are clipped to servo limits.
$2N_rN_c$ tile angles become $3L$ focal coordinates. Learned control and placement set the focal points.

The rest of the dissertation builds on one abstraction. Instead of the user position, all tiles of a segment aim at a shared virtual focal point.

Here the array has three segments, each with its own focal point. When a focal point moves, all tiles of its segment re-orient together through the bisector rule; the front view shows their tilt.

Click: a segment of n tiles is controlled by three coordinates instead of 2n angles. For 63 tiles and three segments that is 126 angles versus nine coordinates. Learned control learns these focal points; placement optimizes them.

Ray-traced testbed: Blender scenes + NVIDIA Sionna RT

  • Blender builds walls, AP, UEs, and tiles; a geometric solver sets the tile angles from the focal points
  • XML scene with ITU-R P.2040 materials: $\epsilon_r = a_{\mathrm m} f_c^{\,b_{\mathrm m}}$, $\sigma_c = c_{\mathrm m} f_c^{\,d_{\mathrm m}}$
  • Sionna RT: specular reflection, diffraction, diffuse scattering; received power returned as the training reward or the placement objective
  • 28 GHz (geometry), 60 GHz (learned control), 5.18 GHz (placement): each study has its own scene and baselines, so dB gains are not compared across parts
All results are ray-traced simulation results; no hardware deployment is reported.

Limits of the geometric rule motivate learned control

1. Servo limits
The rule assumes unconstrained rotation; it gives no configuration when the required angles are out of range.
2. Phase
It aligns path directions but not lengths; relative phases are uncontrolled. Not addressed later: it remains a limitation.
3. One static user
It serves one UE at a known position, not several moving users sharing an array.
Answered (WHAT): the tile array and the bisector rule steer signals around corners: +53.0 / +87.5 dB for one static user.
Opens (HOW): how to act within servo limits, optimize received power rather than geometric alignment, and serve several moving users? → learned focal points.

The study also exposes three limits. First, servo limits: the rule assumes unconstrained rotation, as the red tiles in the animation showed.

Second, phase: it aligns directions but not path lengths, a likely cause of the shortfall at T-shaped locations 2 and 5. No later part controls phase; it stays a limitation.

Third, it serves one user at a known position. The next part keeps the bisector rule but learns the focal points, within servo limits, trained on received power, for several moving users. Its baselines are static reflectors and learned variants, not a position-tracking geometric rule.
